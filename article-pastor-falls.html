When a Man of God Falls, What Happens to the People Who Loved Him?

A personal reflection on grief, pastoral failure, accountability, and learning to rest our faith in Christ rather than the people who proclaim Him.

September 30, 2026

When a Man of God Falls, What Happens to the People Who Loved Him?

I never knew Steven Lawson personally.

But I loved his preaching.

And maybe that's why his fall affected me as much as it did.

I was actually going through his series on Romans when the news came out about his adultery.

Let's call it what it was.

Adultery.

I remember feeling absolutely crushed.

Not disappointed.

Not merely surprised.

Crushed.

There was something deeply unsettling about watching a man whose sermons had fed me spiritually suddenly fall into something so serious.

And then came a thought that frightened me:

If a man who has preached the Word for so many years can fall like this, what is stopping a simple woman like me from falling?

That question stayed with me.

I think I may have even gone into a short period of depression.

Because suddenly, the people I had looked up to didn't seem as secure as I had imagined them to be.

I was angry

And honestly?

At first, I was angry at everyone else.

His sermons were removed.

His ministry was stopped.

Everything seemed to happen so quickly.

It felt like we weren't even given time to grieve.

One day, his sermons were there.

Then they weren't.

It was almost as if he had been erased from the internet.

I remember wondering:

Was this right?

Shouldn't there have been more grace?

Shouldn't there have been more time?

What about all the people who had been genuinely helped by his preaching?

What about people like me?

I wasn't defending adultery.

I knew it was wrong.

But I was grieving.

And I don't think I understood then that grieving the fall of a pastor and believing that he should be removed from ministry are not contradictory things.

I didn't understand that yet.

Then, two years later, it happened again.

Except this time, it wasn't Steven Lawson.

It was a pastor here in India.

A Reformed pastor whose preaching I loved.

Someone much closer to home.

Someone whose ministry wasn't something I encountered through a screen, but someone connected to a church community I knew.

And once again, there was sexual sin.

He had been travelling around the world without his wife.

And eventually, we found out why.

He had been living in sin for more than a year.

He confessed.

But then something happened that I found incredibly difficult to process.

He turned around and accused the people confronting him of forcing a confession out of him.

I remember thinking:

What?

And somehow, some people believed him.

Some left with him.

And eventually, he was asked to step away from the church and not return.

But instead of simply stepping away, he began a Bible study in his home with those who had left with him.

And suddenly, I felt like I was watching the same story again.

Except this time, I was watching it from the inside.

These weren't distant names on the internet.

These were people we knew.

People we loved.

People we prayed with.

And it hurt in a completely different way.

And then I understood something I hadn't understood two years earlier.

I understood why the church had acted the way it did with Lawson.

I understood why a man cannot simply return to the pulpit because he is gifted.

I understood why the church has to take pastoral qualifications seriously.

Because the Bible doesn't say:

"An overseer should be a really good preacher."

It says he must be above reproach.

He must be the husband of one wife.

His character matters.

His home matters.

His reputation matters.

His faithfulness matters.

The qualifications in 1 Timothy 3 and Titus 1 aren't suggestions for an ideal pastor.

They are qualifications for the office.

And suddenly, something that had once seemed harsh to me started making sense.

A good sermon doesn't qualify a man for the pulpit.

This is probably one of the hardest things I've had to learn.

A man can preach brilliantly.

He can explain Scripture beautifully.

He can write books.

He can have decades of ministry behind him.

He can preach Romans in a way that makes you fall in love with the doctrines of grace all over again.

And he can still fall into terrible sin.

Because giftedness and godliness are not the same thing.

Knowledge and character are not the same thing.

A ministry platform is not proof of spiritual maturity.

And a man's ability to preach God's Word does not give him permission to violate God's Word in his own life.

That is painful to admit.

Especially when you loved his preaching.

But here's where I had to learn another difficult distinction.

Forgiveness is not the same thing as restoration to office.

A man can repent.

A man can be forgiven.

A man can be restored to fellowship with God's people.

And yet that does not necessarily mean he should return to pastoral ministry.

I think I used to see removal from ministry as almost the opposite of grace.

Now I don't.

Sometimes grace looks like telling the truth.

Sometimes grace looks like consequences.

Sometimes grace looks like protecting the sheep.

Sometimes grace looks like saying:

"We love you, but you cannot continue in this office."

That's not the church saying that God's grace isn't big enough.

It's the church taking God's qualifications seriously.

And yet, I still don't want to become cynical.

That's important to me.

Because there's another danger here.

When a pastor falls, it is easy to become suspicious of every pastor.

To think every man is secretly hiding something.

To assume every ministry is fake.

To stop trusting anyone.

I don't want to go there.

The answer to discovering that a shepherd can fall isn't to stop believing in shepherds.

It's to remember that Christ is the Chief Shepherd.

Pastors are under-shepherds.

They are sinners.

They need grace.

They need accountability.

They need brothers who will tell them the truth.

They need churches willing to confront them when they sin.

And they need to remember that standing behind a pulpit doesn't make them incapable of falling.

And perhaps that's what scared me the most in the beginning.

Not just that he fell.

But that I could fall too.

That thought still scares me.

I don't want to look at someone else's sin and think,

"I would never do that."

Because Scripture gives us a much more sobering warning:

"Let anyone who thinks that he stands take heed lest he fall."
— 1 Corinthians 10:12

So my prayer has changed.

I don't pray,

"Lord, make me the kind of Christian who could never fall."

I pray,

"Lord, keep me."

Keep me humble.

Keep me close to Your Word.

Keep me accountable.

Keep me from secret sin.

Keep me from loving something more than I love You.

Keep me from thinking that years of Christianity make me immune to temptation.

Keep me from believing that I am stronger than the people who have fallen.

Because I'm not.

I need grace just as much as they do.

There is still something I am grateful for.

I don't personally know everything that happened behind the scenes in Steven Lawson's confession.

And I don't want to pretend that I do.

But at least, from what was publicly acknowledged, there was an admission of the adultery.

And I've learned that confession matters.

It doesn't erase consequences.

It doesn't automatically restore someone to ministry.

It doesn't undo the damage.

But there is something very different between saying,

"I sinned."

and saying,

"You forced me to admit that I sinned."

One is an acknowledgment of guilt.

The other shifts the focus onto the people confronting the sin.

And watching those two situations unfold has made that distinction painfully clear to me.

Two years ago, I was asking, "Why did they remove him?"

Now I'm asking a different question.

"Why would we ever expect them not to?"

Not because I don't believe in grace.

But because I believe the qualifications God gave for pastors actually mean something.

Above reproach.

Husband of one wife.

Faithful.

Self-controlled.

Respectable.

Able to teach.

Not arrogant.

Not violent.

Not greedy.

A man whose life can withstand examination.

Those qualifications aren't there because pastors are supposed to be perfect.

They're there because pastors are supposed to be qualified.

And when serious, disqualifying sin becomes evident, the church has a responsibility to take that seriously.

I don't know what the future holds for either man.

And perhaps it isn't my place to determine it.

God knows their hearts far better than I ever could.

My responsibility is different.

To learn.

To grieve.

To pray.

To take sin seriously.

To take grace seriously.

To never put a pastor where only Christ belongs.

Because that's ultimately what I had to learn.

I loved the sermons.

I loved the teaching.

I was helped by the ministry.

And none of that needs to be rewritten.

God can use a man's preaching even when that man later proves unfaithful.

The truth he preached doesn't become false because the preacher sinned.

The gospel doesn't collapse because the messenger failed to live up to it.

The Bible doesn't become less true because the man preaching it fell.

The treasure was never the vessel.

The treasure was Christ.

And maybe that's why, after two very painful years, I'm finally able to say something I couldn't say when I first heard the news:

I can grieve the fall of a man without questioning the faithfulness of God.

I can mourn what happened.

I can be angry about sin.

I can have compassion for a sinner.

I can believe in forgiveness.

I can believe in repentance.

I can believe that God restores broken people.

And I can also believe that a man may no longer be qualified to stand behind the pulpit.

Those things aren't contradictory.

And perhaps the most important lesson of all is this:

Don't build your faith on the man behind the pulpit.

Build it on the Christ he is supposed to proclaim.

Because men will fail.

Even men who have preached for decades.

Even men whose sermons changed our lives.

Even men we loved.

But Christ?

Christ will not fail.
