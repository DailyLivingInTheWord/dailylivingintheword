When the Shepherd Becomes the Source of the Rumours

When pastoral influence is used to spread stories, protect sin, and silence uncomfortable questions, what happens to the flock?

September 30, 2026

There is something deeply disturbing about watching a pastor fall.

But there is another kind of fall that doesn't always make headlines.

It doesn't necessarily involve adultery.

It doesn't always involve a secret life being exposed.

Sometimes it looks like something much quieter.

A story being told that isn't true.

A person's reputation being slowly dismantled.

A conversation happening behind closed doors.

A half-truth being repeated until it becomes "fact."

People being told things about someone who isn't there to defend themselves.

And eventually, the story becomes so widely repeated that people stop asking whether it was ever true in the first place.

What makes this especially grievous is when it comes from a pastor.

Because when an ordinary person gossips, it is sinful.

When a pastor does it, something more is at stake.

The influence of the pulpit has now entered the conversation.

Gossip Isn't Harmless

I think Christians sometimes have a strange relationship with gossip.

We condemn it when it is obvious.

But we excuse it when it is dressed up as concern.

"I'm only telling you so you can pray."

"You need to know what happened."

"I'm just warning people."

"I'm concerned about the church."

"I'm only sharing my side."

But changing the vocabulary doesn't change the nature of the act.

If what is being shared is untrue, exaggerated, deliberately misleading, or information that has no legitimate reason to be disclosed, calling it "concern" doesn't make it righteous.

Scripture is remarkably direct about this.

Proverbs says:

"A dishonest man spreads strife, and a whisperer separates close friends." — Proverbs 16:28

And James doesn't treat the tongue as a small problem.

He says the tongue can be like a fire.

That should frighten us.

Because words don't disappear after they are spoken.

A rumour can travel farther than the truth.

A lie can reach ten people before the person being lied about even knows it was said.

And by the time the truth catches up, some people have already made up their minds.

But What Happens When the Person Spreading It Is the Pastor?

This is where the situation becomes particularly painful.

A pastor is supposed to shepherd people toward truth.

He is supposed to protect the flock.

He is supposed to be trustworthy.

He is supposed to be someone whose words can be weighed seriously because his life and doctrine are supposed to withstand scrutiny.

And yet, what happens when that same influence is used to spread accusations?

What happens when a pastor tells people things about another believer that aren't true?

What happens when he presents one side of a conflict as though it were the whole truth?

What happens when he quietly creates a narrative about someone and then watches that narrative spread through the church?

That isn't merely interpersonal conflict anymore.

It becomes a shepherding problem.

Because people trust pastors.

And that trust carries weight.

The Most Painful Part Wasn't Just What Was Said

It was what was being protected.

Because while all of this was happening, there was another pastor whose serious sin had already come to light.

And instead of allowing the situation to be dealt with honestly, the narrative seemed to shift.

Suddenly, attention was being directed elsewhere.

Questions about the sin became questions about the people who confronted it.

Those who asked uncomfortable questions became the problem.

Those who wanted accountability were portrayed negatively.

And the person who had sinned was defended.

That is an incredibly dangerous pattern.

Because now the issue isn't simply:

"A pastor sinned."

It becomes:

"What will we do to protect him from the consequences of that sin?"

And sometimes, sadly, protecting the reputation of a pastor becomes more important than protecting the truth.

But Scripture Doesn't Give Pastors That Freedom

A pastor isn't called to protect another pastor's reputation at the expense of truth.

He isn't called to manipulate a narrative because the truth is uncomfortable.

He isn't called to silence people who ask legitimate questions.

And he certainly isn't given permission to destroy another person's reputation in order to make someone else look better.

Ephesians 4:25 says:

"Therefore, having put away falsehood, let each one of you speak the truth with his neighbor." — Ephesians 4:25

Not speak what is convenient.

Not speak what protects your friend.

Not speak what preserves your ministry.

Speak the truth.

There Is a Difference Between Confronting Sin and Spreading Gossip

This distinction matters.

The Bible absolutely commands believers to confront sin.

Matthew 18 gives a process for dealing with serious wrongdoing.

Paul instructed churches to deal with serious sin.

Church discipline is biblical.

Accountability is biblical.

Warning people about genuine danger can be biblical.

But none of that gives us permission to abandon truthfulness.

If someone has sinned, deal with the sin.

If an accusation is made, establish the facts.

If someone needs to be confronted, confront them.

If a matter needs to be brought before church leadership, bring it there.

But don't turn the church into a courtroom where the verdict is reached through whispers.

And don't turn a pastor's office into a place where reputations are decided by private conversations.

And Then There Is the Question of "Covering"

Christians sometimes misunderstand what it means to "cover" someone's sin.

Love does not mean pretending sin didn't happen.

Grace does not mean protecting someone from every consequence.

Forgiveness does not mean refusing accountability.

And loyalty does not mean lying for someone.

There is a world of difference between covering a repentant sinner with grace and covering sin so that the sinner doesn't have to face the consequences.

One looks like the gospel.

The other can become complicity.

If a pastor knows another pastor has sinned and his response is to help hide it, minimize it, attack those who expose it, or construct a different story to protect him, that isn't pastoral care.

That is a serious spiritual problem.

What Makes This Especially Heartbreaking Is the Damage to Ordinary People

Pastors aren't the only ones affected when pastors fight.

Church members are.

Families are.

Friendships are.

People who trusted their leaders become confused.

People who ask questions can be isolated.

People who speak the truth can suddenly find themselves being treated as divisive.

And people who don't know the full story are left trying to figure out what happened.

Meanwhile, the rumour continues travelling.

This is how churches become divided.

Not always through one explosive event.

Sometimes through hundreds of little conversations.

One person says something.

Another repeats it.

Someone adds a detail.

Someone else interprets it.

And eventually, a story exists that nobody can trace back to its original source.

But everyone "knows" it.

I Have Learned That Not Every Accusation Deserves to Be Believed

And not every confident person is telling the truth.

That might sound obvious.

But when the person speaking is a pastor, it becomes much harder.

Because we naturally assume:

"Why would a pastor say this if it wasn't true?"

That question is precisely why pastors have such enormous responsibility with their words.

A pastor's influence should make him more careful, not less careful.

He should be slower to speak.

More committed to facts.

More willing to say, "I don't know."

More willing to hear both sides.

More willing to correct himself when he is wrong.

And more fearful of damaging someone's reputation through careless speech.

The Bible Actually Warns Us About This

James 3 says that not many should become teachers because teachers will be judged more strictly.

That verse isn't only about theological accuracy.

It should make anyone who teaches God's people tremble.

Because influence carries responsibility.

The more people trust you, the more damage your words can cause when those words are false.

A pastor doesn't get a larger margin for error because people respect him.

He gets a greater responsibility because people respect him.

I Don't Want Revenge

I don't want anyone destroyed.

I don't want another pastor's family destroyed.

I don't want people cheering when a Christian leader falls.

That's not what this is about.

Sin should grieve us.

Even when the sinner has hurt us.

Even when we believe someone has lied about us.

Even when we believe someone has protected another person's sin.

The goal shouldn't be humiliation.

The goal should be truth.

Repentance.

Restoration where restoration is biblically possible.

And the protection of God's people.

But Truth Matters Too Much to Remain Silent About It

There comes a point when silence stops being peace.

There is a difference between refusing to participate in gossip and refusing to tell the truth.

There is a difference between forgiving someone and pretending they didn't do anything wrong.

There is a difference between humility and allowing yourself to be falsely accused without ever speaking.

And there is a difference between protecting someone's dignity and protecting their sin.

I've had to learn those distinctions the hard way.

What Should a Shepherd Do When Another Shepherd Falls?

Tell the truth.

Deal with the sin.

Protect the flock.

Call for repentance.

Submit the matter to proper accountability.

Don't manipulate the congregation.

Don't create enemies out of the people who ask questions.

Don't spread stories you cannot substantiate.

Don't use the pulpit, private conversations, or pastoral influence to destroy someone's reputation.

And don't confuse loyalty with covering sin.

Because eventually, the truth comes out.

It always does.

Maybe not immediately.

Maybe not in the way we expect.

Maybe not while the people involved are still around to see it.

But God sees.

And that is ultimately what gives me peace.

I don't have to win every argument.

I don't have to convince everyone.

I don't have to chase every rumour.

I don't have to defend myself against every story being told.

God knows what is true.

A Pastor Is Not Above Accountability

That may be one of the hardest lessons I have learned.

We are often taught to honour pastors—and rightly so.

But honouring a pastor doesn't mean putting him beyond examination.

Respecting spiritual authority doesn't mean surrendering discernment.

And submitting to church leadership doesn't mean believing every statement made by church leadership without question.

The Bereans were commended because they examined what they were taught against Scripture.

Pastors are not exempt from that standard.

In fact, they should welcome it.

A healthy shepherd shouldn't be threatened by truth.

He should love it.

I Still Believe the Church Is Worth Fighting For

Not fighting for a personality.

Not fighting for a particular pastor.

Not fighting for a denomination.

Not fighting for someone's reputation.

Fighting for truth.

Fighting for integrity.

Fighting for the kind of church where sin is confronted rather than concealed.

Where repentance is genuine rather than performed.

Where accusations are investigated rather than whispered.

Where pastors are accountable.

Where victims aren't automatically treated as troublemakers.

Where loyalty to a friend never becomes more important than loyalty to Christ.

And where the shepherds remember that they too are sheep under the authority of the Chief Shepherd.

Because one of the saddest things a church can experience isn't simply a pastor who falls into sin.

It is a church leadership culture that teaches people to protect the fallen pastor, attack the people who confront the sin, and call that protection "grace."

That isn't grace.

Grace tells the truth.

Grace confronts sin.

Grace calls sinners to repentance.

Grace doesn't need lies to protect itself.

And ultimately, the church doesn't belong to any pastor.

It belongs to Christ.

So let the truth be told.

Let sin be called sin.

Let repentance be real.

Let accountability be genuine.

Let gossip die.

Let lies be exposed.

And let the church once again become a place where the reputation of Christ matters more than the reputation of His servants.
