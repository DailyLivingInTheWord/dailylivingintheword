When the Shepherd Becomes the Source of the Rumours

When pastoral influence is used to spread stories, protect sin, and silence uncomfortable questions, what happens to the flock?

September 30, 2026

When the Shepherd Becomes the Source of the Rumours

There is something deeply disturbing about watching a pastor fall.

But not every fall begins with a scandal that makes headlines.

Sometimes, it begins with words.

A story being told that isn't true.

A person's reputation being slowly dismantled.

A half-truth being repeated until it becomes accepted as fact.

Conversations happening behind closed doors.

People being told things about someone who isn't there to defend themselves.

And eventually, the story becomes so widely repeated that people stop asking whether it was ever true in the first place.

What makes this especially grievous is when it comes from a pastor.

Because when an ordinary person gossips, it is sinful.

When a pastor does it, something more is at stake.

The influence of the pulpit has now entered the conversation.

Gossip isn't harmless

I think Christians sometimes have a strange relationship with gossip.

We condemn it when it is obvious. But we excuse it when it is dressed up as concern.

"I'm only telling you so you can pray."

"You need to know what happened."

"I'm just warning people."

"I'm concerned about the church."

"I'm only sharing my side."

But changing the vocabulary doesn't change the nature of the act.

If what is being shared is untrue, deliberately misleading, or needlessly damaging to another person's reputation, calling it concern doesn't make it righteous.

Scripture is remarkably direct about this.

Proverbs 16:28 says:

"A dishonest man spreads strife, and a whisperer separates close friends."

And James doesn't treat the tongue as a small problem. In James 3, he describes the tongue as a fire capable of causing enormous destruction.

That should make us stop and think.

Words don't disappear after they are spoken.

A rumour can travel farther than the truth.

A lie can reach ten people before the person being lied about even knows it was said.

And by the time the truth catches up, some people have already made up their minds.

Relationships have been damaged. Friendships have been lost. Trust has been broken.

All because someone thought their words didn't matter.

But what happens when the person spreading the rumours is the pastor?

This is where the situation becomes particularly painful.

A pastor is supposed to shepherd people toward truth.

He is supposed to be trustworthy.

He is supposed to be an example to the flock, not someone whose conduct the flock must learn to excuse.

And yet, what happens when that same influence is used to spread accusations?

What happens when a pastor tells people things about another believer that aren't true?

What happens when he presents one side of a conflict as though it were the whole truth?

What happens when he creates a narrative about someone and allows that narrative to spread through the church?

That isn't merely an interpersonal disagreement anymore.

It becomes a serious failure of shepherding.

People trust pastors. They listen to them. They give their words considerable weight.

A pastor can influence how an entire congregation views a person, sometimes without that person ever being given an opportunity to respond.

That is an enormous responsibility.

A pastor's position should make him more careful with his words, not less accountable for them.

When gossip becomes a tool

Gossip isn't always an accidental slip of the tongue.

Sometimes it becomes a way of influencing how people think about someone.

A person is described in a particular way. Their motives are questioned. Their actions are presented without context. Their concerns are dismissed. Their character is attacked.

And gradually, people begin to see them through the story they've been told.

Instead of examining the facts, they judge the person.

Instead of asking questions, they draw conclusions.

Instead of seeking reconciliation, they take sides.

And the person at the centre of the rumours may find themselves isolated, misunderstood, or treated as a troublemaker simply because someone else has shaped the narrative about them.

This is one of the most damaging things gossip can do.

It doesn't merely spread information. It can change relationships, divide a church, and destroy a person's reputation.

When a pastor participates in this, the damage can be particularly severe because his position lends credibility to his words.

People may assume that a pastor would never speak carelessly or falsely about someone.

But spiritual authority does not make a person incapable of sin.

And a confident statement from a pastor is not automatically a truthful one.

What does the Bible say about the tongue?

The Bible doesn't treat our words as insignificant.

Ephesians 4:25 commands believers to put away falsehood and speak truthfully to one another.

Ephesians 4:29 says that our speech should build others up according to their needs, giving grace to those who hear.

And Proverbs 12:22 says:

"Lying lips are an abomination to the LORD, but those who act faithfully are his delight."

These are not merely suggestions for polite Christian conversation.

They reveal something about the character God expects from His people.

We cannot claim to love the truth while deliberately misrepresenting someone.

We cannot claim to pursue peace while spreading words that create division.

We cannot claim to care for the church while damaging the people who make up the church through careless or false speech.

And we cannot excuse these things simply because the person doing them happens to be a pastor.

God does not give His servants permission to sin with their tongues.

There is a difference between confronting sin and spreading gossip

This distinction matters.

The Bible commands believers to deal seriously with sin. It calls for accountability, correction, and, where necessary, church discipline.

There are legitimate reasons to raise concerns about a person's conduct.

There are times when people must be warned about genuine wrongdoing.

There are situations that need to be brought before church leadership.

But none of this gives us permission to abandon truthfulness.

If someone has sinned, deal with the actual sin.

If an accusation is made, establish the facts.

If someone needs to be confronted, confront them.

If a matter needs to be brought before church leadership, follow a proper process.

But don't turn the church into a courtroom where the verdict is reached through whispers.

Don't make accusations you cannot substantiate.

Don't present assumptions as facts.

Don't share private information merely because you are angry with someone.

And don't use spiritual language to make gossip sound righteous.

Accountability seeks truth. Gossip spreads a story. They are not the same thing.

A pastor is not above accountability

Perhaps one of the most dangerous assumptions in church life is that questioning a pastor's conduct is automatically disrespectful.

We are taught to honour those who lead us, and rightly so. Scripture speaks of the responsibility of church members toward their leaders.

But honour does not mean pretending that leaders are incapable of wrongdoing.

Respect does not mean abandoning discernment.

And submission does not mean believing every statement made by a church leader without question.

The Bereans were commended in Acts 17:11 because they examined the Scriptures to determine whether what they were being taught was true.

If Christians are encouraged to examine teaching against Scripture, surely we must also take seriously the biblical standards governing the character of those who teach.

In 1 Timothy 3:2, an overseer must be above reproach. James 3:1 warns that those who teach will be judged with greater strictness.

These passages should make anyone entrusted with spiritual leadership examine their own life carefully.

A pastor is not above accountability because he occupies the pulpit.

In fact, his position makes accountability all the more important.

What happens when a pastor refuses to acknowledge the damage?

Everyone can sin with their words.

Every Christian can speak carelessly, repeat something they shouldn't have repeated, or make an unfair judgment about another person.

The question is not whether a pastor has ever made a mistake.

The question is what happens when the wrongdoing is brought to his attention.

Does he listen?

Does he examine the facts?

Does he acknowledge what he said?

Does he correct what was false?

Does he seek to repair the damage?

Or does he justify his behaviour, blame the person who was hurt, and continue as though nothing happened?

There is a significant difference between a man who stumbles with his words and a man who repeatedly refuses to take responsibility for them.

Repentance is not merely saying that everyone makes mistakes.

It is acknowledging one's own sin.

It is telling the truth about what happened.

It is being willing to make things right where possible.

And it is demonstrating a willingness to change.

A pastor who has damaged someone's reputation should not consider correction beneath him.

Humility is not only expected from the people sitting in the pews.

It is expected from the man standing behind the pulpit too.

The damage extends far beyond the person being discussed

Pastors aren't the only ones affected when rumours spread.

Church members are.

Families are.

Friendships are.

People who trusted their leaders become confused.

People who ask questions may find themselves isolated.

People who speak up may suddenly be treated as divisive.

And those who don't know the full story are left trying to understand what happened.

This is how churches become divided.

Not always through one explosive event.

Sometimes through hundreds of little conversations.

One person says something.

Another repeats it.

Someone adds a detail.

Someone else draws a conclusion.

And eventually, a story exists that nobody can trace back to its original source.

But everyone seems to know it.

The tragedy is that the church, which should be a place where truth is spoken in love, can become a place where people are afraid of what might be said about them when they leave the room.

That should grieve us.

I don't want revenge. I want truth.

When someone has spoken falsely about you, the temptation to retaliate can be strong.

You want everyone to know what really happened.

You want the person who lied to be exposed.

You want those who believed the rumour to understand that they were wrong.

You want your reputation restored.

Those feelings are understandable.

But I don't want revenge to become the purpose.

I don't want anyone destroyed.

I don't want people cheering when a Christian leader falls.

I want truth.

I want repentance.

I want accountability.

I want people to understand the seriousness of their words.

And I want the church to become a place where people can raise legitimate concerns without being subjected to character assassination.

There is a difference between seeking justice and seeking revenge.

There is a difference between correcting a falsehood and spreading another one.

There is a difference between defending yourself and allowing bitterness to determine how you treat others.

Romans 12:18 tells us, as far as it depends on us, to live peaceably with everyone.

Sometimes pursuing peace requires an honest conversation.

Sometimes it requires correcting the record.

Sometimes it requires appropriate church accountability.

And sometimes, despite our best efforts, reconciliation may not be possible.

But whatever happens, we are not given permission to abandon the truth.

God sees what people say in secret

One of the hardest things about rumours is that you cannot always correct them.

You may never know who heard what.

You may never get an opportunity to explain yourself.

Some people may believe a story about you without ever asking you what happened.

Others may continue believing it even after the facts are clarified.

That is painful.

But it is also where we must remember that our lives are ultimately lived before God.

He knows what is true.

He knows what was said.

He knows what was misunderstood.

He knows what was deliberately misrepresented.

And He knows the hearts of everyone involved.

That doesn't mean we should never seek to correct wrongdoing. It doesn't mean we should remain silent when someone is being harmed.

It means we don't have to become consumed by the need to convince every person of our innocence.

We can pursue truth faithfully and leave the final judgment to God.

Our responsibility is to walk in truth. God's judgment does not depend on whether everyone believes us.

The shepherd must remember whose flock it is

The church does not belong to the pastor.

It belongs to Christ.

The people sitting in the pews are not instruments to be used to protect a leader's reputation, settle personal disputes, or punish those with whom he disagrees.

They are people Christ calls His own.

A shepherd is entrusted with their care.

That responsibility should produce humility, not entitlement.

It should produce careful speech, not careless accusations.

It should produce a willingness to listen, not a determination to control the narrative.

And it should produce a fear of God that makes a man think carefully before speaking about another person's character.

A pastor will answer to the Chief Shepherd for the way he has carried out his responsibilities.

That is a sobering thought.

I still believe the church is worth fighting for

Not fighting for a personality.

Not fighting for a particular pastor.

Not fighting for someone's reputation at the expense of the truth.

Fighting for integrity.

Fighting for a church where truth matters.

Where accusations are examined rather than whispered.

Where people are corrected fairly.

Where repentance is genuine.

Where leaders are accountable.

Where Christians refuse to participate in gossip, even when the gossip comes from someone they respect.

And where the reputation of Christ matters more than the pride of His servants.

I don't expect perfection from pastors.

I expect them to take God's Word seriously enough to acknowledge their sin when they fail.

I expect them to understand that spiritual leadership is a responsibility, not a licence to speak without accountability.

And I believe that the church must be willing to address these failures rather than excuse them.

Because a pastor's calling does not make his words harmless.

His influence makes them consequential.

Let us be people of truth

If you are a pastor, be careful with your words.

If you hear a rumour, don't automatically repeat it.

If someone shares an accusation, don't assume it is true simply because the person speaking is confident or respected.

If you have spoken falsely about someone, correct it.

If you have damaged a reputation, take responsibility for your part in that damage.

If you have been hurt by gossip, pursue truth without allowing bitterness to consume you.

And if you are tempted to join a conversation that tears another person down, remember that you will never honour Christ by participating in something He condemns.

Let the church be a place where truth is spoken in love.

Let gossip die.

Let falsehood be corrected.

Let accountability be genuine.

Let repentance be real.

And let pastors remember that they, too, are under the authority of the Word they preach.

The church does not need perfect shepherds. It needs shepherds who take holiness, truth, and accountability seriously.

And above all, it needs people who remember that the reputation of Christ matters far more than the reputation of any man who stands behind His pulpit.
